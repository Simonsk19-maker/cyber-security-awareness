Social engineering

The tricks that fool the person, not the computer — and how to see them coming.

Most attacks don't start by breaking a computer — they start by fooling a person. That's social engineering: a convincing message or call that gets someone to click, pay, or hand over a password. The good news is that once you know the tricks, they're much easier to spot.

The buttons they push

Nearly every scam leans on one or more of these feelings. If a message is pushing them hard, slow down:

  • Urgency — “act now”, “final demand”, “your account closes today”.
  • Authority — pretending to be the boss, your bank, IT support or a supplier.
  • Fear — a fine, a hacked account, a problem you must fix immediately.
  • Helpfulness — playing on your wish to be helpful (“just do me a quick favour”).
  • Curiosity or reward — a prize, a refund, a juicy-looking attachment.
  • Trust & familiarity — using a name, logo or detail that looks genuine.

The common forms

TrickWhat it looks like
Phishing (email)A fake email with a bad link or attachment.
Smishing (text)A scam text — fake delivery, bank or refund.
Vishing (phone call)“Your bank” or “Microsoft” calls about a problem and asks for codes or remote access.
PretextingA made-up story to win trust — “I'm the new IT engineer, I just need your password to fix it”.
BaitingA free download, or a USB stick left lying around, that installs malware.
Quishing (QR codes)A QR code (on a poster, email or parking meter) that leads to a fake login page.
TailgatingSomeone following you through a secure door, or “forgot my pass, can you let me in?”

How to resist — the simple defences

  • Slow down. Urgency is the number-one tool. Genuine requests survive a pause.
  • Verify independently. Contact the person or company on a number/email you already have — never the details in the message.
  • Never give passwords, login codes or remote access to someone who contacted you.
  • It's always OK to say “let me check” — to anyone, including someone claiming to be the boss.
  • Don't plug in unknown USB sticks, and be wary of scanning unexpected QR codes.
Build a little culture of it: agree a rule that money and password requests are always double-checked by phone or in person, make reporting easy and blame-free, and remind the team that the fraudster is counting on no one wanting to question “the boss”.
